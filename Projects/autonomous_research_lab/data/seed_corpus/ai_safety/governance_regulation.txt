AI Governance and Regulation Landscape

Government approaches to AI regulation broadly split into risk-tiered
frameworks and use-case-specific rules. The EU AI Act classifies systems
into unacceptable-risk (banned outright), high-risk (subject to
conformity assessments, documentation, and human oversight
requirements), limited-risk (transparency obligations, such as
disclosing AI-generated content), and minimal-risk categories. High-risk
classification applies to systems used in areas like employment
screening, credit scoring, and critical infrastructure.

In the United States, regulation has so far been more fragmented,
combining sector-specific rules (e.g., FDA oversight of AI-based medical
devices), state-level legislation, and voluntary frameworks like NIST's
AI Risk Management Framework. Executive orders have directed federal
agencies to develop safety testing requirements for the most capable
frontier models, including mandatory reporting of training runs above
certain compute thresholds.

A central governance debate concerns evaluation and auditing: who
verifies that a frontier model has been adequately tested for dangerous
capabilities (bioweapons uplift, cyberweapon capability, autonomous
replication) before deployment, and what standard of evidence should be
required. Third-party auditing bodies and government-run AI safety
institutes have emerged as one proposed answer, though their legal
authority and technical capacity vary significantly by jurisdiction.
