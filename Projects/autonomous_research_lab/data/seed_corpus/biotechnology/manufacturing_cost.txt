Manufacturing and Cost Challenges in Cell and Gene Therapy

Unlike traditional small-molecule drugs, many cell and gene therapies
are manufactured on a per-patient (autologous) basis, which fundamentally
changes the economics of production. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell
therapies, for example, require extracting a patient's own T-cells,
genetically modifying them to express a receptor targeting cancer
cells, expanding them in culture, and reinfusing them — a process that
can take several weeks and cannot be easily scaled the way batch
manufacturing of a chemical drug can.

Allogeneic ("off-the-shelf") approaches, which use donor cells modified
to reduce immune rejection risk, are being developed specifically to
address this scalability problem, potentially enabling one manufacturing
batch to treat many patients. However, allogeneic approaches introduce
new technical challenges, particularly around preventing both
graft-versus-host disease and host-versus-graft rejection.

The high manufacturing complexity contributes directly to list prices
that can exceed $1 million per treatment for some approved gene
therapies. This has driven experimentation with outcomes-based payment
models, where insurers pay in installments contingent on the therapy
continuing to show efficacy over time, rather than a single upfront
payment — shifting some of the financial risk of therapy durability
back toward manufacturers.
