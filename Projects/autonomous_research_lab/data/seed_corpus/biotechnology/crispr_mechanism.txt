CRISPR Gene Editing: Mechanism and Applications

CRISPR-Cas9 works by pairing a guide RNA sequence with a target DNA
sequence, directing the Cas9 enzyme to cut both strands of DNA at a
precise location. The cell's natural repair machinery then either
disrupts the gene (non-homologous end joining, which introduces small
insertions/deletions) or, if a repair template is supplied, inserts a
specific new sequence (homology-directed repair).

Newer variants address precision limitations of the original system.
Base editing uses a modified Cas9 that nicks rather than fully cuts DNA,
paired with an enzyme that directly converts one DNA base to another
without creating a double-strand break, reducing unwanted insertions
and deletions. Prime editing goes further, using a Cas9 nickase fused
to a reverse transcriptase, allowing precise insertions, deletions, and
all twelve possible base-to-base conversions without double-strand
breaks or a separate repair template.

Approved therapeutic applications include ex vivo treatments for sickle
cell disease and beta-thalassemia, where a patient's own hematopoietic
stem cells are edited outside the body and reinfused. In vivo editing
(editing cells directly inside the patient) remains more challenging
due to delivery constraints — lipid nanoparticles and viral vectors
each have different tissue-targeting limitations and immunogenicity
profiles.
