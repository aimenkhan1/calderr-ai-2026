Carbon Pricing Mechanisms Compared

Carbon pricing takes two primary forms. A carbon tax sets a fixed price
per ton of CO2 emitted, giving emitters price certainty but no certainty
about the resulting emissions quantity — actual emissions reductions
depend on how responsive emitters turn out to be to that price signal.
A cap-and-trade (emissions trading) system instead sets a fixed cap on
total emissions and allows the market to determine the price through
trading of a limited number of allowances, giving quantity certainty
but price volatility.

The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), the largest carbon market
globally, covers power generation, industry, and (as of recent reforms)
maritime shipping, using a declining cap that reduces the total number
of allowances issued each year to drive down emissions over time. A
carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM) has been introduced alongside
it, applying a comparable carbon cost to certain imported goods to
prevent "carbon leakage" — production simply relocating to
jurisdictions without a carbon price rather than emissions actually
falling.

A persistent design challenge in both approaches is the treatment of
carbon offsets — credits generated by emissions-reducing projects
outside the capped sector, such as reforestation or methane capture at
landfills. Offset quality varies enormously in practice; the central
technical debate is over "additionality" (would the reduction have
happened anyway without the offset revenue) and permanence (particularly
for forestry offsets, which can be reversed by fire or logging).
