Direct Air Capture: Technology and Economics

Direct air capture (DAC) removes carbon dioxide directly from ambient
air, as opposed to point-source capture, which captures CO2 at
concentrated emission sources like power plants. Two dominant DAC
approaches exist. Liquid solvent DAC uses a hydroxide solution to
chemically bind CO2 from air passed through the solvent, then applies
heat (typically requiring temperatures above 800°C) to release
concentrated CO2 and regenerate the solvent. Solid sorbent DAC instead
uses a solid material with chemically reactive surfaces that bind CO2
at ambient temperature, then releases it under a combination of mild
heat and vacuum.

Energy intensity is the central economic constraint. Because atmospheric
CO2 concentration is roughly 420 parts per million — far lower than at a
power plant flue — DAC requires processing enormous volumes of air per
ton of CO2 captured, and both approaches require significant thermal or
electrical energy for the regeneration step. Current cost estimates
range widely, from roughly $250 to $600 per ton of CO2 captured for
first-of-a-kind commercial facilities, with cost-reduction roadmaps
targeting closer to $100-150 per ton as manufacturing scales and
next-generation sorbents improve capture efficiency.

Captured CO2 has two primary end uses: permanent geological
sequestration in deep saline aquifers or depleted oil reservoirs
(generating carbon removal credits), or utilization in synthetic fuel
production, concrete curing, or enhanced oil recovery — though the last
of these is contested as a legitimate climate solution since it
typically produces more oil to be burned.
