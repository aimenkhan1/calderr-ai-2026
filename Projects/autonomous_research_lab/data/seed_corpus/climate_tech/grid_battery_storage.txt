Grid-Scale Battery Storage: Chemistries and Duration

Lithium-ion remains the dominant grid-scale storage chemistry today,
primarily using lithium iron phosphate (LFP) formulations for
stationary storage rather than the nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC)
chemistries favored in electric vehicles, since LFP trades some energy
density for lower cost, longer cycle life, and better thermal
stability — properties that matter more for a stationary installation
than for a vehicle where weight is critical.

Lithium-ion battery storage is economically well-suited to short-duration
applications (roughly 2-6 hours of discharge), covering daily solar
generation mismatches and providing grid frequency regulation. Longer
duration needs — multi-day storage to cover extended low-renewable-output
periods — are generally considered uneconomical for lithium-ion at
current costs, driving research into alternative "long-duration energy
storage" (LDES) technologies.

Leading LDES candidates include iron-air batteries (using reversible
rusting of iron, targeting roughly 100-hour discharge duration at low
per-kWh cost using cheap and abundant materials), flow batteries
(storing energy in liquid electrolyte tanks, where duration is
determined by tank size rather than the amount of power-conversion
hardware, decoupling power and energy scaling), and various forms of
thermal and gravitational storage. As of the mid-2020s, most LDES
technologies remain at pilot or early-commercial scale, with lithium-ion
still handling the large majority of deployed grid storage capacity.
