Quantum Error Correction and the Path to Fault Tolerance

Physical qubits are noisy — gate operations, measurement, and even idle
qubits accumulate errors far faster than classical bits do. Quantum
error correction (QEC) addresses this by encoding one "logical" qubit
across many physical qubits in a way that allows errors to be detected
and corrected without directly measuring (and thus destroying) the
quantum information being protected — a constraint that makes QEC
fundamentally harder than classical error correction.

The surface code is the most widely pursued QEC scheme for
superconducting and related architectures, arranging physical qubits in
a 2D lattice where neighboring qubits are repeatedly measured in a
pattern that reveals error syndromes without collapsing the encoded
logical state. Surface code error correction has a threshold property:
below a certain physical error rate per gate, adding more physical
qubits per logical qubit exponentially suppresses the logical error
rate; above that threshold, adding more qubits makes things worse, not
better — making crossing the threshold a critical experimental
milestone.

The overhead is substantial: published estimates suggest roughly 1,000
or more physical qubits may be required per logical qubit at
near-threshold error rates to achieve error rates low enough for
commercially relevant algorithms (such as breaking RSA encryption or
simulating industrially relevant molecules), meaning a fault-tolerant
machine running practically useful algorithms may require millions of
physical qubits — several orders of magnitude beyond current systems,
which typically have on the order of hundreds of physical qubits.
