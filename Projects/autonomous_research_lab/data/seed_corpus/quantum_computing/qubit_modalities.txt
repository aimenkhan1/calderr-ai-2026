Qubit Modalities Compared

Different physical implementations of qubits trade off differently
across coherence time, gate fidelity, connectivity, and manufacturing
maturity. Superconducting qubits, used by several leading commercial
efforts, are fabricated using established semiconductor lithography
techniques and operate at millikelvin temperatures. They offer fast gate
speeds (tens of nanoseconds) but relatively short coherence times
(microseconds), and fixed physical connectivity between neighboring
qubits on the chip, which constrains which two-qubit operations can be
performed directly.

Trapped-ion qubits use individual charged atoms held in
electromagnetic fields, manipulated with laser pulses. They offer
significantly longer coherence times (seconds) and, because ions in a
chain can all interact with each other via shared motional modes,
effectively all-to-all connectivity within a chain — avoiding the
routing overhead superconducting architectures face. The tradeoff is
slower gate speeds (microseconds) and current scaling challenges in
chaining together many ions while maintaining gate fidelity.

Neutral atom arrays, a newer approach, use optical tweezers to arrange
individual atoms in reconfigurable 2D or 3D patterns, offering both
large qubit counts and flexible connectivity by physically rearranging
atom positions between operations. Photonic qubits, encoding
information in light, offer natural room-temperature operation and
straightforward networking between separate processors via optical
fiber, but face different challenges around deterministic single-photon
generation and detection efficiency. No modality has yet demonstrated a
clear overall advantage across all relevant metrics simultaneously.
