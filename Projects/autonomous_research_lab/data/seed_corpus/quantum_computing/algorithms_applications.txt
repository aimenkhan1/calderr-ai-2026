Quantum Algorithms and Realistic Near-Term Applications

Shor's algorithm, which factors large integers exponentially faster
than the best known classical algorithms, is the most famous quantum
algorithm because it threatens the security of RSA and elliptic-curve
cryptography — but running it on cryptographically relevant key sizes
requires a fault-tolerant quantum computer with millions of physical
qubits, which does not yet exist. This is the primary driver behind
"post-quantum cryptography" standardization efforts, which aim to
deploy classical encryption algorithms believed to resist quantum
attack well before large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers arrive.

Grover's algorithm offers a quadratic (not exponential) speedup for
unstructured search problems, which is a smaller advantage but applies
to a broader class of problems and requires far fewer qubits than
Shor's algorithm to become practically relevant.

In the current "noisy intermediate-scale quantum" (NISQ) era, before
full fault tolerance is achieved, the most actively pursued near-term
applications are quantum simulation of molecular and material systems
(where the exponential complexity of simulating quantum systems on
classical computers is a natural fit for a quantum computer, particularly
for problems in catalysis and battery materials research) and certain
optimization problems via variational quantum algorithms, which use a
classical optimizer in a feedback loop with a shallow quantum circuit.
Claims of "quantum advantage" on NISQ hardware remain contested, since
classical algorithms have repeatedly been improved to match specific
claimed quantum speedups after the fact.
